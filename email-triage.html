Turning Linguistic Clues into an Email-Triage Workflow

Unsolicited emails can demand more attention than they deserve. Invitations to interviews, book reviews, promotional services or editorial collaborations may sound plausible at first, yet turn out to be generic, poorly targeted or unlikely to lead anywhere. Repeatedly assessing them one by one became a problem of attention as much as email management.

I developed a personal Gmail filter to automate part of that first-pass judgment. The script combines linguistic, contextual and sender-related clues, translating recurring observations into explicit rules and a weighted score. Its purpose is to reduce the effort spent reviewing low-credibility or low-relevance solicitations—not to determine with certainty whether a message is fraudulent or AI-generated.

The challenge: more than suspicious keywords

The clues were often relational rather than conclusive in isolation. A French message might contain a literal-sounding translation, switch unexpectedly into an English sign-off, refer to several books when the known context involved one, or include a reference that seemed unrelated or fabricated. Other messages showed inconsistencies between a sender’s display name and signature, or between the sender’s domain and links in the email.

These patterns were observations from a personal inbox, not a representative dataset. They helped define a practical question: how could recurring linguistic and contextual judgments be expressed as rules a script could apply consistently?

From observation to executable rules

Using JavaScript in Google Apps Script, I built a Gmail workflow that retrieves unread inbox conversations in batches, examines selected fields from the latest message, and applies a series of heuristic checks. Regular expressions identify configured language patterns; conditional logic compares selected message details, including sender information, the recipient field, signatures and domains.

The rules include literal-translation patterns in French, unexpected English phrases in otherwise French messages, mismatches in references to the number of books or works, and selected identity or domain inconsistencies. Each detected pattern contributes a defined number of points. When the total reaches the configured threshold, the documented V3 script moves the conversation to Trash and records the execution.

Why the approach is distinctive

The project connects close linguistic reading with practical system design. Rather than relying on a single suspicious word, it formalizes several kinds of evidence—language, context and message metadata—into a decision process. The rules are visible and adjustable, so the logic can be inspected and refined as new examples or edge cases emerge.

It also illustrates a broader skill: converting qualitative observations into criteria that a technical workflow can apply. Linguistic judgment informs the rules, while scripting makes those rules repeatable.

Scope and limitations

This is a rule-based personal tool, not a trained machine-learning model, a validated phishing detector or a reliable way to attribute authorship to an AI system. Awkward phrasing, code-switching or contextual errors can occur for many reasons; they are signals to consider, not proof of origin or intent. The score represents configured rules triggered by a message, not a statistical probability of fraud or a validated measure of commercial relevance.

The documented V3 implementation processes unread conversations in the inbox, skips threads containing more than two messages and analyses the latest message rather than the full thread. Reaching the threshold triggers a move to Trash without human confirmation, so a legitimate message could be affected. A separate correction for Gmail aliases is documented, but its integration into the executed version has not been confirmed. Available records do not establish a measured accuracy rate, false-positive rate, total volume processed or time saved.

What the project demonstrates

  • Identifying and categorizing linguistic and contextual anomalies in Canadian French correspondence.
  • Translating qualitative observations into explicit conditions, regular expressions and weighted decision rules.
  • Applying JavaScript and Google Apps Script to automate a recurring Gmail workflow.
  • Considering edge cases, false positives and the difference between a useful signal and a proven conclusion.
